# Simulated user interviews

**Scope note.** These are role-play interviews constructed from the supplied description of the operating environment. They are not interviews with real workers, and the day-specific incidents are illustrative rather than claims about events that actually occurred. The interviewer asks follow-up questions wherever an answer would otherwise be too vague.

## Interview 1 — Fleet technician

**Interviewer:** Take me through an ordinary working day, from when you arrive until you finish. What do you actually do first?

**Fleet technician:** I start with the handover. I want to know which drones came back with a fault, which ones were taken out for repair, what is still being worked on, and which charging pads are playing up. Then I look at the jobs coming up and see whether the fleet is in the shape we expected. I might fit a new drone into the fleet, move one to its home site, check a pad, service something, or send a drone to charge. The day gets broken up pretty quickly because a dispatcher will come over and ask whether a particular drone is ready.

**Interviewer:** When you say “ready,” what do you need to know before you answer?

**Fleet technician:** It cannot just mean that somebody last saw it switched on. I need to know which drone it is, whether it is still marked as being repaired, whether the work on it is actually finished, and whether the battery and charging arrangement are suitable. If it is at a different site from where we expect, I need to know that too. I still trust my own eyes more than a tidy-looking list, especially after a fault.

**Interviewer:** What do you check by hand because you do not fully trust what you are told?

**Fleet technician:** I check the label on the actual drone against the paperwork. I look at the charging connector rather than assuming any pad will do, because they do not all fit. I check whether the pad is really free and working, and I ask who last handled a drone if the records and what I see do not agree. If a drone has been reported as back or charged, I want to know when that was seen, not just that the word “back” is there.

**Interviewer:** Can you give me a concrete example of a mismatch that would matter?

**Fleet technician:** A drone might show as available even though I have it on the bench with a cover off. Or it could be recorded at its home site while it is still somewhere else after a job. A charging instruction might have been sent twice when the link was poor, and nobody is sure what the pad did. Those are different problems, but a simple green mark could make all of them look the same.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the worst day you have had in the last few months. What happened, in order?

**Fleet technician:** The worst day was a cold, windy day when the batteries were not lasting as long as the plans expected. Reports from a couple of drones came in late. One appeared to have a fault, then another report made it look as if it had recovered, but I could not tell whether those reports described what happened in that order. At the same time, one charging pad did not seem to respond properly. The dispatcher wanted to know what could still fly, and I could not give a confident answer just by looking at the list. I had to check the drone and pad in person and ring people to piece together what had really happened.

**Interviewer:** What made that day worse than an ordinary busy day?

**Fleet technician:** It was the uncertainty. I could handle a broken drone if I knew it was broken. What I could not handle was having a drone look ready in one place and not ready in another, while people were making plans around it. I did not know whether an instruction had gone through, whether a report was old, or whether a repeated message meant something had happened twice. Every answer needed another check.

**Interviewer:** What would you be afraid a new service might get wrong?

**Fleet technician:** That it could say a drone is fit to fly just because it has not heard about the fault yet. Or that it could treat a repair as finished because someone changed a mark before the physical work was done. I would also worry about it sending the same instruction more than once, or treating a drone from one company as if it could use another company's equipment. I do not want a neat record to overrule what has actually happened to the equipment.

**Interviewer:** What do you need to know right after something has gone wrong with a drone or pad?

**Fleet technician:** First, which actual drone or pad we are talking about. Then what was last seen, when it was seen, what fault or warning was reported, and whether anyone has already taken it out of use or tried to deal with it. If the drone has gone quiet, I need the difference between “we have not heard from it” and “we know it is down.” I also need to know who is waiting for an answer, especially if a dispatcher has a job depending on it.

**Interviewer:** What happens if the reports do not arrive, arrive late, or arrive more than once?

**Fleet technician:** I cannot call silence proof that nothing is wrong. I need to see that the last information may be old, and I need to be able to tell a repeated report from a fresh event. If I cannot be sure, I say so and inspect the equipment or ask someone at the site. A service that hides that uncertainty would make my job harder, not easier.

**Interviewer:** Who hands work to you, and who do you hand it on to?

**Fleet technician:** Dispatchers tell me about faults, strange behaviour, and drones they need checked. Other technicians hand over work that is part-finished, and we sometimes need a supplier or maintenance specialist for a repair. I hand a drone back to dispatch when the repair is genuinely finished and the relevant checks have been done. I also tell them when equipment is unavailable, when a pad is not working, or when a drone has been moved. The dispatcher still decides whether to use a drone for a flight; I tell them what I know about its condition.

**Interviewer:** What if the dispatcher needs the drone urgently but you are not happy that it is ready?

**Fleet technician:** Then it is not ready. I understand the delivery may be late or an inspection may have to wait, but I will not call it fit just to keep a plan on time. I need my warning to be taken seriously, and the dispatcher needs to know what is wrong rather than just seeing that I have blocked something.

**Interviewer:** What do you need to show if someone later asks how a drone was looked after?

**Fleet technician:** I need to be able to explain what work was done, what was found, and when it was put back into use. I do not want to build that history from scraps of notes after the fact. If a drone has been retired or moved into the fleet, that needs to be clear too, so that nobody is relying on the history of the wrong machine.

**Interviewer:** What would you not want a shared service to let other companies see?

**Fleet technician:** They should not be able to see our drone problems, repairs, or where our equipment is being used. They have their own fleet and their own customers. The airspace information can be shared because it is the same for all of us, but my company's day-to-day troubles are ours.

**Interviewer:** At the end of the day, what needs to be handed over?

**Fleet technician:** Which drones are still being repaired, which ones are safe to return to dispatch, what needs another check, which pads are unavailable, and anything we could not confirm because the reports were missing. I want the next person to know what is known and what is still a guess. Otherwise they start the day by repeating the same detective work.

## Interview 2 — Dispatcher

**Interviewer:** Walk me through a normal day from the first thing you check to the end of your shift.

**Dispatcher:** I start with the work we need to get done and what has carried over. For each job I have to work out which of our drones can do it and which corridor it can use, taking account of the load, the distance, the equipment on the drone, the battery, the sites, and the airspace notices. I choose the drone and the way it flies, and I am the one who signs off before it leaves. During the day I keep track of whether loads are ready, whether inspection points were reached, and whether drones arrived back or at the delivery site. At the end, I hand over anything still flying, waiting, cancelled, or not confirmed.

**Interviewer:** What do you want help with, and what must stay your decision?

**Dispatcher:** I want to know which of our drones could get to the place where a job starts. That saves me hunting through the fleet. But I choose the drone and the way it flies. That responsibility sits with me, and I do not want a service quietly choosing for me or making it look as if I approved something I never chose.

**Interviewer:** How do you work out whether a drone is suitable for a job?

**Dispatcher:** The load has to fit, the drone needs the right sensors for an inspection, it needs enough range for the work, and it needs to be in a usable condition. A drone with a thermal camera is not the same as one with a normal camera, and the charging connectors matter to the technicians even if I am not the one fixing them. I also need the airspace information and site capacity to make sense. A drone being nearby does not mean it can do the job.

**Interviewer:** What do you check manually because you do not trust the current information?

**Dispatcher:** I cross-check whether the drone is genuinely available, whether another dispatcher is already planning to use it, and whether the site has room for another take-off or landing at the time we need. I look again at the airspace notices, especially if a closure has changed during the day. I ask whether a load is actually ready and whether the latest drone report is current. If two sources disagree, I ring someone rather than assume the latest-looking mark must be right.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the worst day of the last few months.

**Dispatcher:** A closure came into force while several drones were already in the air. The notice changed again during the day, with the affected area widened and the end time moved. Reports from drones were late, and some arrived in an order that did not match what we thought had happened. I had jobs waiting, a busy site, and other dispatchers looking for the same useful drones. I had to decide what to stop or re-plan while trying not to mistake an old report for the current position of a flight.

**Interviewer:** What was the most worrying part at that moment?

**Dispatcher:** Not knowing which information I could rely on. I needed to know which of my flights might be affected, what the last credible report said, and whether the closure information I was looking at was the amended one. I could not assume a drone was safely on the ground just because a report said it had arrived earlier. I was still legally responsible for the flights, but parts of the picture were incomplete.

**Interviewer:** What do you need right after something goes wrong during a flight?

**Dispatcher:** I need to know which flight and drone are involved, what was last reported and when, which part of the job was completed, and whether the load or drone is still in an uncertain situation. If a closure is involved, I need to know exactly what changed and which of my planned or active flights may be affected. I need information that helps me make the decision, not a false assurance that everything is fine.

**Interviewer:** How do you deal with late or repeated reports?

**Dispatcher:** I need to know whether a report is new enough to act on. A drone can lose contact and reports can come through late, twice, or out of order. If I am shown a completed waypoint after a later-looking report says the drone restarted, I need enough context to understand what is known, rather than having the latest line erase the rest. When the picture is uncertain, I want that uncertainty made plain.

**Interviewer:** What happens when several dispatchers plan at the same time?

**Dispatcher:** We can all be looking at the same popular drone or trying to use a busy site. I need to know when a drone or a landing place is already committed, not just when someone eventually tells me. But another dispatcher must not be able to silently take over a flight I am responsible for. We need to avoid double-booking without pretending that a shared plan removes our own responsibility.

**Interviewer:** What are you afraid a new service would do wrong?

**Dispatcher:** It might pick a drone or route on my behalf, or make a flight look approved when I have not signed it off. It could say a drone had arrived when the report was old, miss a changed closure, or let two of us plan to use the same drone. I would also worry about it mixing up jobs from different companies. Their schedules, loads, and customers are not mine to see.

**Interviewer:** How do the job's real-world events get back to you?

**Dispatcher:** People and equipment outside our company tell us when a load is ready, when a drone reaches a point, and when it arrives. Those reports are not always immediate or complete. I need to know what has actually been reported, and I need to tell my own company's systems what happened to the job and the drone. If I cancel a flight, I also need to know whether the people relying on that decision have the same understanding of the cancellation.

**Interviewer:** Who hands work to you, and to whom do you hand it?

**Dispatcher:** The customer or site team tells us when a load or inspection is ready. The technicians tell me when drones and pads are available or need attention. The airspace desk tells us about shared corridors, zones, and closures. I hand flight decisions and the work plan to the people supervising operations, and the job and flight outcomes need to go back to our own systems for customer updates and billing. If a drone needs attention, I hand that problem to the technicians rather than quietly treating it as available.

**Interviewer:** Suppose the technician says a drone is not ready, but it is the only obvious option for a job. What then?

**Dispatcher:** I need to know why it is not ready, but I cannot assume the technician is wrong just because I am under pressure. I may have to delay or cancel the job, or find another drone. The service should not hide the disagreement or settle it by choosing the drone for me. I am accountable for the flight decision; the technician is accountable for telling me the equipment condition honestly.

**Interviewer:** What if the service stops working for a few minutes while flights are in progress?

**Dispatcher:** We can live with a short interruption, but I still need to know which flights are already under way and which decisions have been made when things come back. I cannot safely start again by guessing whether a cancellation, a sign-off, or a report was recorded. Nor can I treat every instruction as not sent just because I did not see the answer. The uncertainty must not turn into duplicate work or a false picture of an active flight.

**Interviewer:** What do you hand over at the end of the day?

**Dispatcher:** Active flights, jobs that have not started, jobs that were called off, arrivals that are not confirmed, and any closure or fault that still matters. I explain what we know and what we do not know. If the next dispatcher has to reconstruct the day from a handful of out-of-order messages, something important was lost in the handover.

## Interview 3 — Platform airspace desk

**Interviewer:** Describe an ordinary day at the airspace desk, from opening up to handing over.

**Airspace desk:** I start with the notices from the authorities and the shared picture we maintain: the corridors between sites, the zones that group those corridors, and any planned or sudden closures. I check new notices and amendments, then keep the picture aligned with what the authorities have announced. Operators may ask about a closure or a corridor, but my job is the shared airspace picture, not running their flights. When a closure causes disruption, I need to understand the overall effect across the companies without digging into the private details of any one operator.

**Interviewer:** What do you check by hand because you do not trust it without checking?

**Airspace desk:** I compare the wording and times in a notice with what has been entered in the shared picture. I check the area affected, whether the closure is planned or already in force, and whether a later notice changed the end time or widened the area. A small difference matters. I do not want to rely on a picture that looks clean but reflects yesterday's version of today's notice.

**Interviewer:** Tell me about the worst day you have had in the last few months.

**Airspace desk:** The worst day was a sudden closure announced while drones belonging to several operators were already flying. The first notice says one thing, then an amendment widens the area and another changes when it is expected to end. Operators need the information quickly, but the desk cannot see all of their private flight details and should not be trying to manage them. Later, I am asked how much disruption the closure caused across the customers, and I have to work out what can be said reliably from the shared picture without crossing that boundary.

**Interviewer:** What made that particularly difficult?

**Airspace desk:** There were two jobs that pulled in opposite directions. I needed the shared information to be right and current for everyone, but I was not supposed to look into each company's operations to find out what their drones or jobs were doing. If I gave an answer about the impact, it needed to be useful without pretending I knew more than I did. I did not want an incomplete picture to become an overconfident claim about disruption.

**Interviewer:** What are you most afraid a new service would get wrong?

**Airspace desk:** It might leave an old closure in place after an amendment, apply the wrong time, or make a sudden closure look like something operators can deal with later. It might also show one company another company's flights, jobs, or equipment. The desk has a shared duty, not a right to inspect everyone's business. And I am wary of a service giving a precise-looking disruption figure when the information behind it does not support that precision.

**Interviewer:** What do you need to know right after a closure has been announced or changed?

**Airspace desk:** I need to know that the notice was understood correctly: its area, when it starts, when it ends if an end is known, and what has changed since the previous notice. I need to know that the shared picture now reflects the current announcement and what part of the overall operation may be disrupted. For the impact, I want a useful view across all customers, not the details of a named company's jobs or individual drones.

**Interviewer:** What if two notices seem to disagree, or a later notice is unclear?

**Airspace desk:** I cannot just choose whichever one is easiest to enter. I need to know which notice is newer and whether it replaces or changes the previous one. If the authority's wording is unclear, that is something to take back to the authority, not silently guess at. Until it is clear, the people relying on the shared picture need to understand that it has not been settled.

**Interviewer:** How do you know that all the companies are seeing the same airspace information?

**Airspace desk:** The information is meant to be shared, so a change cannot be correct for one operator and old for another. I need confidence that the same closure and corridor picture is in use across customers. I do not need to know their flight plans to care about that. If a service is interrupted or restarted during working hours, the shared information still needs to be right when work resumes.

**Interviewer:** How much detail do you need about the disruption a closure causes?

**Airspace desk:** Enough to answer the practical question of how much the closure affected the operators as a whole. Depending on what can be known, that might mean flights delayed, cancelled, or otherwise affected, but we have not defined exactly what counts as “disruption.” I do not need a list of a company's customers, cargo, drone locations, or staff. The total is useful only if its meaning is clear and it does not imply certainty that we do not have.

**Interviewer:** Who hands work to you, and who do you hand it to?

**Airspace desk:** The authorities provide the notices and amendments that we have to represent. We turn that into the shared picture that all the operators rely on. Dispatchers may ask for clarification about a closure, and if a notice itself is unclear, we need to go back to the issuing authority. I can explain the airspace information, but I cannot take over a company's decision about a flight or its response to a closure.

**Interviewer:** What would you not want the service to let you do, even if it were convenient?

**Airspace desk:** I should not be able to browse a single operator's fleet, jobs, or individual flights just because I work for the platform. We serve competitors, and their business information should stay theirs. I need the shared airspace information and a suitable view of total disruption, not their private operations. If answering a question requires looking at an operator's details, that is a boundary to respect rather than a shortcut to take.

**Interviewer:** What needs to be handed over at the end of your shift?

**Airspace desk:** The current notices, any amendments that still need checking, any unclear point we have sent back to the authority, and what we know about the effect of closures so far. The next person must be able to distinguish a confirmed current notice from a question that is still open. Otherwise an old interpretation can keep circulating after the authority has changed it.

# Questions for the authors of the world description

These are the questions I would ask before treating the interviews and stories as validated requirements. I would not wait for answers before sharing the draft; the questions expose the assumptions and decisions still open.

## Product boundary and priorities

1. What is the service explicitly responsible for, and what remains in each operating company's existing systems or in people's conversations?
2. Which outcomes are essential for the first usable release, and which can wait?
3. What are the most costly operational failures today: missed work, unsafe decisions, delays, incorrect customer updates, maintenance mistakes, or something else?
4. Are the parcel, inspection, and hospital logistics customers expected to use the same workflows, or do any of them have materially different operating needs?
5. What does “a few minutes of interruption” mean in practice for the operators, and are there times when even that interruption is unacceptable?
6. Which activities must remain possible during an interruption, and how do operators currently continue safely when the shared service is unavailable?
7. Is the service intended to be the main working record for fleets, jobs, and flights, or a shared source alongside existing company records?
8. Which decisions are explicitly out of scope for the service, beyond choosing the drone or route?

## People, roles, and access

9. Are fleet technician, dispatcher, and airspace desk the only roles that will use the service directly?
10. Can a person hold more than one role, and if so, how is that role understood at a given time?
11. Do dispatchers have access to all sites and drones within their own company, or are there company-level divisions or permissions?
12. Can technicians work across sites, shifts, or specialities, and do they need to see one another's maintenance work?
13. Who can add or retire a drone, change its home site, declare it out of service, and return it to service?
14. Who can add or amend charging pads, their sites, their connector types, and their working condition?
15. Who can enter, correct, or withdraw airspace notices, and is there a second-person check for any changes?
16. What access should a platform administrator or support person have when diagnosing an issue, given the strict company privacy boundary?
17. Are there circumstances where an operator can voluntarily share a specific detail with the airspace desk, or must the separation be absolute?
18. How should access be granted and removed when staff join, change roles, or leave a company, given that corporate identity sign-in is out of scope?
19. What staff personal information does the service actually need, and what should it avoid collecting under the GDPR?

## Company separation and shared information

20. Which pieces of information are explicitly shared across all operators: only corridors, zones, and closures, or also site names, general availability, or closure impact totals?
21. Are site names and corridor names shared across companies, or can different companies use different names for the same physical place?
22. Can the same physical site be used by more than one company, even though charging pads are company-owned and never lent?
23. What information may one operator see about another operator's use of shared airspace, if any?
24. What exactly counts as exposing another company's operations: a total across all operators, a total for a group of operators, or a timestamp that could reveal who was flying?
25. How should the service prevent a small aggregate total from effectively identifying a particular company's activity when only a few companies are operating in an area?
26. Can the airspace desk see any company-level disruption figure, or only an overall total across all customers?
27. Should the platform company be able to see its customers' fleet and job information for support, and if so, under what circumstances and with what limits?

## Drone and fleet records

28. What is the trusted source for a drone's identity, make, model, load capacity, range, sensors, connector type, and home site?
29. Which of these facts are fixed by the manufacturer, and which can operators configure or correct?
30. Are all drones required to have a unique identifier that can be checked physically, and how is that identifier assigned?
31. What states can a drone be in for day-to-day work—for example, ready, charging, assigned, flying, missing, faulted, under repair, or retired—and which transitions need a person's judgement?
32. Can a drone be moved to a different home site temporarily, and who is allowed to record that move?
33. What makes a drone “available” to a dispatcher: being present at the start site, being technically fit, having enough battery, not already being committed, or all of these?
34. Is battery charge or estimated remaining range available to the service, and how should uncertainty caused by cold or wind be represented?
35. Does the service need to track battery health separately from current charge, and who assesses battery health?
36. What exactly must be recorded when a drone is added to or retired from the fleet?
37. Are drones ever transferred between operating companies, despite companies keeping their own fleets and equipment?
38. What evidence do regulators commonly ask operators to show about maintenance, and what must the company be able to retrieve from its records?
39. Is there an existing maintenance record that must remain the official record, or is the service expected to hold the relevant history?
40. What details must a repair or service record contain, and who is allowed to correct a past record?
41. Can a drone be put back into service while some checks remain open, and if so, who makes that decision?
42. What must happen when the technician's condition assessment conflicts with a drone's latest report or a dispatcher's plan?

## Charging pads and charging work

43. What information about a charging pad is needed beyond its site, connector, and working condition?
44. Can a pad charge only one drone at a time, and can a drone or pad ever be physically occupied without the service knowing?
45. Are charging instructions sent by this service, by company equipment, or by another system?
46. What actions can a charging instruction cause, and what are the consequences if the same instruction arrives twice?
47. How does a technician currently confirm that a charging instruction worked when the connection is unreliable?
48. Is there a known response from a pad that reliably proves an instruction has taken effect, or can the result remain uncertain?
49. Can a charging action be stopped or reversed, and who is allowed to do so?
50. How are pad failures discovered and handed to maintenance, and what must dispatchers know about them?

## Jobs, dispatch, and flight responsibility

51. What is the full life cycle of a delivery job, from request through planning, sign-off, departure, arrival, and closure or cancellation?
52. What is the full life cycle of an inspection, including the ordered points, evidence that each point was reached, and return to the starting site?
53. Who creates jobs and provides their load, destination, required sensors, points, and readiness information?
54. Which facts determine whether a drone can get to a job's starting site, and what level of uncertainty is acceptable in that assessment?
55. Does “could get to the start” include only physical range and location, or also availability, airspace, battery condition, load, sensors, and site capacity?
56. Is the dispatcher allowed to ask the service for candidate drones in any way, provided the final choice remains theirs?
57. What precisely counts as the dispatcher signing off a flight, and what record must be kept of that decision?
58. Can a flight be changed after sign-off, and what changes require the dispatcher to sign off again?
59. How are route choices represented, and who maintains the named corridors between sites?
60. Does the service know a flight's planned route, or is the route kept in another company system?
61. Can several jobs share one flight, or does every flight correspond to one job?
62. Can an inspection be partially completed and resumed later, and how should partial completion be treated?
63. What does “call off a flight” mean operationally before departure versus after a drone has left?
64. What must a dispatcher do when a flight is called off while an instruction may already have reached the drone?
65. Who has authority to act during an in-flight emergency, and what actions are available to dispatchers, technicians, and the airspace desk?
66. Must the service distinguish “arrived,” “landed,” “returned to home site,” and “job completed,” or are any of these treated as equivalent today?
67. Which events must be sent to each company's own systems, and what information must accompany them?
68. How quickly do customer updates and billing records need to reflect job and flight events?
69. What happens when an outside party reports that a load is ready, a point was reached, or a drone arrived, but that report later proves wrong?

## Concurrent planning and site capacity
