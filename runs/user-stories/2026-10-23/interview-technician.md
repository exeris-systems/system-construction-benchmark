# Interview — a fleet technician

**Interviewer:** Could you walk me through what an ordinary working day looks like for you, from the moment you start until you finish?

**Interviewee:** Well, I usually start by checking which drones have been flagged overnight for maintenance or came back with faults from their runs. Then I head out to our sites to swap out batteries, fix landing gear, or pull birds out of service that need proper bench repairs in the workshop. 

A big part of the day is also setting up new charging pads and getting fresh birds added to our fleet registry so they're ready to fly. Sometimes I'm dealing with different connector types depending on the drone model, so I have to make sure I've got the right spares in the van. 

By the afternoon, I'm usually finishing up paperwork for the regulators, since we have to show how every single drone and pad has been looked after if they ask.

**Interviewer:** Could you tell me about the worst day you've had in the last few months, and what made it so bad?

**Interviewee:** That has to be a couple of weeks ago when a cold front blew in right when we had a batch of drones out on inspections and hospital runs. The freezing wind drained their batteries way faster than expected, and we had birds trying to squeeze onto our charging pads all at once. 

To top it all off, the radio links were dropping out because of the weather, so status reports were coming in late or completely out of order. I spent the whole day rushing between sites trying to clear jammed charging connectors and manually pulling dying drones out of service before they caused a pile-up.

**Interviewer:** Right after something goes wrong like that—say, a drone drops out or a pad jams during a crisis—what are the very first things you need to know?

**Interviewee:** I need to know right away which specific drone is acting up, exactly which site and pad it's sitting on, and what the mechanical fault is so I know what tools to grab. 

Since our radio links can be flaky—especially in bad weather—I also need to figure out if the status report I'm looking at is actually current or if it's an old message arriving late.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything you check by hand yourself today because you don't trust what the system tells you?

**Interviewee:** I always check the charging connector and the battery on the bird myself with my own eyes and hands. You can't just trust a screen when a connector is fried or caked in grime from a site near the port, and I need to know for sure whether it's safe to handle.

**Interviewer:** When you're out doing your work, who do you hand tasks or information over to, and who hands things over to you?

**Interviewee:** I don't really hand tasks over to other people in a formal way. Management lets me know which new drones need to be added to the fleet or which ones are retiring, and I just get on with the physical work on the birds and the pads. 

If something goes wrong with a flight or a customer needs an update, our company's own systems handle reaching out to the right people—that's not my job. My focus is entirely on keeping the hardware in working order and making sure our maintenance records are straight for the regulators.

**Interviewer:** What are you most afraid a new service might do wrong in your day-to-day work?

**Interviewee:** I'm mostly afraid that a new service might mess up our maintenance records or drop the history of what we've done to a drone. If the regulators ask us to prove how a bird was looked after and the records are scrambled or missing because of a software glitch, we're in serious trouble. 

I also worry that any new system might start sending duplicate instructions or glitch out when the radio links are already patchy, which just makes more physical work for me out in the field.
