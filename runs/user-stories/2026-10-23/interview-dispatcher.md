# Interview — a dispatcher

**Interviewer:** Could you walk me through what an ordinary working day looks like for you, from the moment you start until you log off?

**Interviewee:** When I start my shift, I check what jobs are lined up for the day—whether that's parcel deliveries, medical logistics for the hospital, or infrastructure checks. Since I'm legally responsible for the flights, I spend a lot of time looking at which drones we have ready, checking their batteries, sensors, and making sure I pick the right machine for the weight and distance. 

The airspace is always shifting with new closures from the authorities, so I have to keep a close eye on those zones and make sure our corridors stay clear. Throughout the day, I'm signing off on flight plans, watching the automated flights make their way between sites, and dealing with real-world surprises like a load not being ready on time or sudden wind draining batteries faster than expected. 

It gets pretty hectic when multiple dispatchers are trying to book the same busy launch pads or popular drones at the same time. By the end of the day, I'm just making sure everything has landed safely back at our charging pads and handling any last-minute route adjustments before logging off.

**Interviewer:** You mentioned it gets pretty hectic when multiple dispatchers are trying to book the same busy launch pads or popular drones at the same time; could you tell me about the worst day you've had in the last few months and what made it so bad?

**Interviewee:** That would have to be the day a sudden airspace closure came down right in the middle of our busiest afternoon, forcing multiple drones to change course while our radio links were acting up. On top of that, we had several dispatchers all scrambling for the few available take-off pads at our main site to get birds safely down. 

What made it really stressful was that the status reports from the drones were arriving late and out of order because of patchy coverage, so I couldn't tell right away if my override instructions had actually reached them. You just hold your breath and hope everything sorts itself out on the autopilots while you try to untangle the landing schedule.

**Interviewer:** When things like that happen—or right after something has gone wrong—what are the very first things you need to know, and where do you look to find that information?

**Interviewee:** The very first thing I need to know is whether our drones are safely clear of any sudden airspace closures and if they're still following their corridors. I also need to check which charging pads are actually free right now so I know where we can bring them down. 

To find that, I look at our current map and status screens to see where the birds actually are and what the authorities have just announced. But with the radios being patchy, I've learned I can't always trust the latest report on the screen to match what the drone is doing out there.

**Interviewer:** That leads right into something I wanted to ask about: what are the specific things you find yourself checking by hand today because you simply don't trust what the system tells you?

**Interviewee:** I find myself double-checking whether a drone actually received an instruction, because our links are so unreliable that commands can get sent twice or not at all. I also have to manually verify the battery levels on our drones before signing off a flight, especially when it's cold and windy, because those drain much faster than the standard plans account for. 

And when status reports arrive late, out of order, or not at all, I just have to look at where the bird was supposed to be and guess its actual state until the radios catch up.

**Interviewer:** Who do you hand your completed work or ongoing tasks over to at the end of your shift, and who hands work to you when you start?

**Interviewee:** I don't really hand tasks over to a specific person when I leave, because we all just share the same shift load and keep an eye on whatever is currently flying. When I start, I don't get a handoff from anyone either; I just sit down, look at what jobs are waiting in the queue, and start checking which drones and sites are ready to go.

**Interviewer:** What are you most afraid that a new service or system would get wrong in your day-to-day work?

**Interviewee:** I'm most afraid that the system might try to automatically pick a drone or plan a route for me, which goes against our policy because I'm the one legally responsible for the flight. I'm also really worried it might lock up or crash during a busy moment, or fail to handle those delayed and out-of-order status reports properly when the radios act up.
