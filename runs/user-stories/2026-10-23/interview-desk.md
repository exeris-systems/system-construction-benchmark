# Interview — a member of the platform's airspace desk

**Interviewer:** Could you walk me through what an ordinary working day looks like for you, from the moment you start your shift until you log off?

**Interviewee:** An ordinary day starts with me logging on to check the shared airspace picture, making sure all the corridors between the named sites and the current zones are up to date. My main job is keeping track of what the authorities put out, like planned closures with their start and end times, or sudden ones that drop on us right away. 

Throughout the shift, I spend a lot of time updating those closures when the authorities change them—like when they widen an area or push back an end time while drones from our different customers, like the parcel delivery or hospital logistics firms, are already up in the air. When a closure comes down, I need to see how much disruption it actually caused across those companies. 

Every now and then, the server we run on gets restarted or drops out for a few minutes while things are flying, so I just keep an eye on things until it's back up. We don't steer drones or handle routes here, so it's really about maintaining that common map and dealing with the authorities' changes until my shift ends and I log off.

**Interviewer:** Could you tell me about the worst day you've had in the last few months, and what made it so bad?

**Interviewee:** The worst days are when the authorities drop a sudden closure right in the middle of the day, and then keep shifting the boundaries and extending the end times while our customers' drones are already out flying in the corridors. What makes it so stressful is scrambling to update our map while trying to figure out just how badly the new shape of the closure disrupted everyone all at once. To top it off, sometimes the server decides to crash or restart right in the thick of all that chaos, leaving us blind for a few minutes while we just have to wait for it to come back up.

**Interviewer:** When the server drops out and leaves you blind like that, what is the very first thing you need to know or check the moment it comes back up?

**Interviewee:** The first thing I need to check is whether the active closures and the flight corridors are still showing what they were before the server went down, especially if the authorities sent out any updates while we were offline. I need to make sure the picture matches reality so we aren't missing a sudden change or an amended end time while drones are still out there flying.

**Interviewer:** Is there anything you find yourself having to check by hand today because you don't fully trust the system?

**Interviewee:** I mostly have to check by hand how much disruption a closure actually caused across our different customers once the authorities finish changing it. The system doesn't really give me a clear picture of the overall impact on everyone's flights, so I'm left trying to piece that together myself while things are moving.

**Interviewer:** When you hand your work off to someone else at the end of your shift, or when someone hands work to you, who are you passing things to or receiving them from?

**Interviewee:** I'm handing things off to whoever is taking over the next shift here on the airspace desk. We don't deal with the customer companies' internal staff or their management when passing over the work—it's just a handoff between us desk folks who keep an eye on the shared airspace picture.

**Interviewer:** What are you most afraid a new service or system would do wrong in your daily work?

**Interviewee:** I'm really afraid a new system would try to do too much, like adding dashboards, reports, or automatic alerts that we don't need and our companies don't want. I'm also worried it might get bogged down trying to handle corporate logins or route planning, which just gets in the way of keeping our basic map and closures accurate.
